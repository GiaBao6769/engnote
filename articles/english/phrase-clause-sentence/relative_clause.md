# Relative clause 

Relative clauses give you more information about someone or something. They are used to combine clauses without repeating information.

A relative clause is introduced by a relative pronoun or a relative adverb.

- A relative pronoun tells more about a person or object noun in a sentence.
- Relative adverbs tell more about the time and location of a noun.

| Relative pronoun[?s] | Purpose | Examples |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Which | For things and animals | The game which I love is Finding Paradise. |
| Who | For people | I know his brother who used to work for NASA. |
| Whom | For people as the object of the relative clause|The person whom you met yesterday is an actor. |
|Whose | For possession | This is the person whose dog was lost. |
| That | For people, things, animals and time | The burger that I ate really hit the spot. |

---

| Relative Adverbs | Purpose | Examples |
|:--|:--|:--|
| When | For time | It was such a bittersweet time when I was young. |
| Where |  For places | This is the park where we first met. |
| Why | For reasons | That is the reason why she left. |

__In formal writing:__

- When = in/on which
- Where = in/at which
- Why = for which

Compound relative pronouns:
- Compound relative pronouns apply universally to a number of people or things. E.g., wherever, whoever, whenever, etc.

## a. Non-defining relative clause

Use: to give extra information about the person or thing. It is not necessary information. You don’t need it to understand who or what is referred to.

Example:

- Bill Gate, who created the Windows operating system, is the richest person in the world.

- Non-defining relative clauses are always set off by commas (one at the beginning and one at the end).

## b. Defining relative clause

Use: to give essential information about someone or something that we need in order to understand what or who is being referred to. 

Examples:

- The cat which I rescued last week was adopted.

- The man and his dog that are walking together work in the army.

You must use that in these cases:

- After indefinite pronouns such as all, everything, something, anything, nothing, none, little, much, few, any, only. E.g., Everything that he said is not true.

- After superlative adjectives or words such as the first, the last, the only. E.g., This is the worst thing that has
ever existed.

- After mixed pronouns both, neither, either.” E.g., Both of my friends that work as engineers don’t bear it out.

- After compound subjects that contain people and animals, such as “a man and a dog,” “a girl and her cat”.

- After “it is/was… that” structure. E.g., It is the person that we met yesterday.

## c. Reduced relative clauses

If a defining relative clause has the pronoun “that,” “which,” or “whom” that functions as an object of the clause, the pronoun can be omitted.

- The guy that I met was nice. = The guy I met was nice.

- The guy that helped me was nice (correct) / The guy helped me was nice (incorrect).

If a defining relative clause has the pronoun that functions as a subject of the relative clause, and the verb is [? in the active voice], it can be reduced to a present participle phrase by omitting the relative pronoun and “be” or auxiliary verb if there is any.

- The man who is driving the car is my friend. => The man driving the car is my neighbor.

- That woman, driving that car, is a famous actress.

For present and past perfect tenses, you can omit the pronoun, replace “have,” “has” or “had” with “having.” You use this to emphasize the prior completion of the activity in the participle phrase.

- She is the singer who has become famous recently. => She is the singer having become famous recently.

- People who have been asked many questions are clever. => People having been asked many questions are clever.

If a relative clause has the pronoun that functions as the subject of the relative clause and “be” followed by a past participle, you can omit the relative pronoun and “be.”

<!--- __This can be combined together in a category for reduced relative clause for active and passive voice__ --->

- The house which was built last year is very modern. &rarr; The house built last year is very modern.

If a relative clause is in the passive voice but is a continuous tense, you can omit the pronoun and “be.”

- The store which is being built by Apple is large. &rarr; The store being built by Apple is large.

If a relative clause has a superlative adjective or word such as “the first,” “the last,” “the only,” you can omit the
relative pronoun, replace the verb with a to-infinitive.

- Columbus is the first person who discovered the Americas. &rarr; Columbus is the first person to discover the Americas.

- He is the smartest guy who studied in this school. &rarr; He is the smartest guy to study in this school.

## d. Preposition placement in relative clause

In everyday English, the preposition is normally placed at the end of the relative clause and the pronoun may be
included or omitted.

In formal English, the preposition is placed before the relative pronoun, and in this case the pronoun cannot be omitted.

For example:

| Everyday English | Formal English |
|:--|:--|
| Is that the person (that/whom) you were talking to? | Is that the person to whom you were talking? |
| This is the house (that/which) he lives in. | This is the town in which he lives. |
| She is the first person (that/whom) he lives with. | She is the first person with whom he lives. |
